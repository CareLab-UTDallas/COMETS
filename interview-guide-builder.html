Build your own interview guide

The domains below are the ground an unstructured clinical interview has to cover. Write, in your own words and your own order, how you would open each one and what you would ask. What you assemble here is a starting guide that is yours, and that you will revise for years until the scaffold falls away.

Read this first. This page does not save or submit anything you type. Finish in one sitting and copy your guide out at the end. You can come back and build it again from scratch as often as you like, but you cannot pause and return to a saved draft, and nothing you write here is stored or sent anywhere.

Setting the frame

Before any content, make clear the referral question and the purpose of the evaluation, the nature of the evaluation and its final product, and the paperwork, consent, cost, and timeline. Then hand the floor to the person.

The presenting problem

Current stressors and difficulties, when they began, whether acute or chronic, any trigger, why an evaluation now, severity and how much functioning varies, the person's own explanation, whether things are improving or worsening, other stakeholders, and any prior evaluation or treatment.

Diagnostic and mental status screen

When the presenting problem points at a diagnostic category, explore its symptoms directly, and this is where a structured interview may plug in. If mental functioning seems impaired, screen orientation, memory, thought content and process, affect, appearance, attention, speech, and rapport.

Medical and biological context

Recent check-up, chronic disease, hospitalizations, head injury, chronic pain, family history of illness, and current and past medications. For any head injury, get loss-of-consciousness duration, the first memory afterward, the location and cause, and the recovery.

Distal developmental context

The family the person grew up in, how discipline and emotion were handled, attachment to parents, the move through adolescence, the history of friendships and romantic relationships, whether they felt liked, a formative event that left a mark, and any history of trauma.

Sociocultural and relational contexts

The larger world the person lives within, gender, socioeconomic status, race, ethnicity and culture, religion, region, and identity, and the values these carry, and then how the person functions with parents, siblings, family, friends and a best friend, partners, and peers or coworkers.

Academic and occupational functioning

History of achievement and satisfaction with it, grades and current standing, study habits, organization and procrastination, attitude toward school or work, any disciplinary history, and long-term goals and the strength of the drive toward them.

Legal and forensic history

Arrests, charges, and convictions, any incarceration, current or past probation or parole, any current or pending litigation, and any current legal involvement. Ask matter-of-factly, since a guarded response is itself informative, and remember this domain connects to validity and secondary gain in forensic and disability contexts.

Personality and socio-emotional functioning

The deepest domain, worked one system at a time. Take each on its own terms rather than all at once.

Habits and daily activities

Sleep, eating, substance use, sexual patterns, exercise, routine, and how leisure and interests are spent.

The experiential system

The felt experience of being. Can the person reach their feelings and express them? What is the general emotionality? Are emotions chronically active, or over-controlled? Any strong images or flashbacks?

The relational system

The internal models the person navigates others by. Their sense of relational value, felt respected and loved versus rejected, security and trust, whether they lean agentic or communal, and how they handle conflict.

The defensive system

How the person filters and copes. Are they guarded or open? What happens at certain topics? Do they rationalize, suppress, or avoid, and how much genuine insight do they show?

The justification system

The language-based beliefs and values through which the person makes meaning. Verbal ability, self-reflection and ego development, self-regulation and conscientiousness, self-criticism and core beliefs, self-efficacy, driving purpose, and overall life satisfaction.

Observing while you interview (AMSIT)

Appearance, behavior, and speech, which you observe. Mood, which you ask about and record in the person's own words, and affect, which you observe. Sensorium, meaning alertness, orientation, attention, and memory. Intellectual function, which you estimate. Thought, meaning process, content, perception, insight, and judgment.
